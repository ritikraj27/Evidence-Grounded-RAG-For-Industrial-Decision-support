**Six Sigma** (**6σ**) is a set of techniques and tools for process improvement. It was introduced by American engineer [Bill Smith](/wiki/Bill_Smith_(Motorola_engineer) "Bill Smith (Motorola engineer)") while working at [Motorola](/wiki/Motorola "Motorola") in 1986.[[1]](#cite_note-ssorigin-1)[[2]](#cite_note-Tennant6-2)

Six Sigma strategies seek to improve [manufacturing](/wiki/Manufacturing "Manufacturing") quality by identifying and removing the causes of [defects](/wiki/Product_defect "Product defect") and minimizing [variability](/wiki/Statistical_dispersion "Statistical dispersion") in manufacturing and [business processes](/wiki/Business_process "Business process"). This is done by using empirical and statistical [quality management](/wiki/Quality_management "Quality management") methods and by hiring people who serve as Six Sigma experts. Each Six Sigma project follows a defined methodology and has specific value targets, such as reducing pollution or increasing [customer satisfaction](/wiki/Customer_satisfaction "Customer satisfaction").

The term *Six Sigma* originates from [statistical quality control](/wiki/Statistical_quality_control "Statistical quality control"), a reference to the fraction of a [normal curve](/wiki/Normal_curve "Normal curve") that lies within six [standard deviations](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") of the [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean"), used to represent a defect rate.

## History

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section's source code: History")]

[Motorola](/wiki/Motorola "Motorola") pioneered Six Sigma, setting a "six sigma" goal for its manufacturing business. It registered Six Sigma as a [service mark](/wiki/Service_mark "Service mark") on June 11, 1991 ([U.S. Service Mark 1,647,704](http://tarr.uspto.gov/servlet/tarr?regser=serial&entry=1647704)); on December 28, 1993, it registered Six Sigma as a [trademark](/wiki/Trademark "Trademark"). In 2005, Motorola attributed over $17 billion in savings to Six Sigma.[[3]](#cite_note-motsaving-3)

[Honeywell](/wiki/Honeywell "Honeywell") and [General Electric](/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric") were also early adopters of Six Sigma. As GE's CEO, in 1995 [Jack Welch](/wiki/Jack_Welch "Jack Welch") made it central to his business strategy.[[4]](#cite_note-ncsu-4) In 1998, GE announced $350 million in cost savings thanks to Six Sigma, which was an important factor in the spread of Six Sigma (this figure later grew to more than $1 billion).[[5]](#cite_note-QD-5) By the late 1990s, about two thirds of the [Fortune 500](/wiki/Fortune_500 "Fortune 500") organizations had begun Six Sigma initiatives with the aim of reducing costs and improving quality.[[6]](#cite_note-Juran-6)

In recent years[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit), some practitioners have combined Six Sigma ideas with [lean manufacturing](/wiki/Lean_manufacturing "Lean manufacturing") to create a methodology named [Lean Six Sigma](/wiki/Lean_Six_Sigma "Lean Six Sigma").[[7]](#cite_note-WalsheHarvey2010-7) The Lean Six Sigma methodology views lean manufacturing, which addresses process flow and waste issues, and Six Sigma, with its focus on variation and design, as complementary disciplines aimed at promoting "business and operational excellence".[[7]](#cite_note-WalsheHarvey2010-7)

In 2011, the [International Organization for Standardization](/wiki/International_Organization_for_Standardization "International Organization for Standardization") (ISO) published the first standard "ISO 13053:2011" defining a Six Sigma process.[[8]](#cite_note-iso-8) Other standards have been created mostly by universities or companies with Six Sigma first-party certification programs.

The upper and lower specification limits (USL and LSL) are at a distance of 6σ from the mean. Normal distribution means that values far away from the mean are extremely unlikely—approximately 1 in a billion too low, and the same too high. Even if the mean were to move right or left by 1.5 standard deviations (also known as a 1.5 sigma shift, colored red and blue), there is still a safety cushion: approximately 3.4 in a million for the side that the distribution has moved towards, and a much smaller number (32 per [quintillion](/wiki/Quintillion "Quintillion")) on the other.

The term Six Sigma comes from [statistics](/wiki/Statistics "Statistics"), specifically from the field of [statistical quality control](/wiki/Statistical_quality_control "Statistical quality control"), which evaluates [process capability](/wiki/Process_capability "Process capability"). Originally, it referred to the ability of manufacturing processes to produce a very high proportion of output within specification. Processes that operate with "six sigma quality" over the short term are assumed to produce long-term defect levels below 3.4 [defects per million opportunities](/wiki/Defects_per_million_opportunities "Defects per million opportunities") (DPMO). The 3.4 dpmo is based on a "shift" of ± 1.5 sigma explained by [Mikel Harry](/wiki/Mikel_Harry "Mikel Harry"). This figure is based on the tolerance in the height of a stack of discs.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)[[10]](#cite_note-ssdefn-10)

Specifically, say that there are six [standard deviations](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation")—represented by the [Greek letter](/wiki/Greek_alphabet "Greek alphabet") σ ([sigma](/wiki/Sigma "Sigma"))—between the [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean")—represented by μ ([mu](/wiki/Mu_(letter) "Mu (letter)"))—and the nearest specification limit. As process standard deviation goes up, or the mean of the process moves away from the center of the tolerance, fewer standard deviations will fit between the mean and the nearest specification limit, decreasing the sigma number and increasing the likelihood of items outside specification. According to a calculation method employed in process capability studies, this means that practically no items (see figure caption) will fail to meet specifications.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)

The calculation of sigma levels for a process data doesn't need normally distributed data. The common approach for handling these distributions is via transformation (Box-Cox or Johnson) to normal; in fact, a criticism of Six Sigma is that practitioners spend a lot of time transforming data from non-normal to normal. However, sigma levels can be determined for process data that has evidence of non-normality[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9) using the [cumulative density function](/wiki/Cumulative_density_function "Cumulative density function") of a better-fitting distribution. The [generalized lambda distribution](/wiki/Generalized_lambda_distribution "Generalized lambda distribution") was proposed in 2004 as a distribution suitable for this purpose.[[11]](#cite_note-11)

## Doctrine

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Doctrine") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section's source code: Doctrine")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Six_sigma-2.svg/120px-Six_sigma-2.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Six_sigma-2.svg)

Six Sigma symbol

Six Sigma asserts that:

- Continuous efforts to achieve stable and predictable process results (e.g., by reducing process [variation](/wiki/Statistical_dispersion "Statistical dispersion")) are of vital importance to business success.
- Manufacturing and business processes have characteristics that can be defined, measured, analyzed, improved, and controlled.
- Achieving sustained quality improvement requires commitment from the entire organization, particularly from top-level management.

Features that set Six Sigma apart from previous quality-improvement initiatives include:

- Focus on achieving measurable and quantifiable financial returns
- Emphasis on management leadership and support
- Commitment to making decisions on the basis of verifiable data and statistical methods rather than assumptions and guesswork

In fact, [lean management](/wiki/Lean_management "Lean management") and Six Sigma share similar methodologies and tools, including the fact that both were influenced by [Japanese](/wiki/Culture_of_Japan "Culture of Japan") business culture. However, lean management primarily focuses on eliminating waste through tools that target organizational efficiencies while integrating a performance improvement system, while Six Sigma focuses on eliminating defects and reducing variation. Both systems are driven by data, though Six Sigma is much more dependent on accurate data.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Six Sigma's implicit goal is to improve all processes but not necessarily to the 3.4 DPMO level. Organizations need to determine an appropriate sigma level for each of their most important processes and strive to achieve these. As a result of this goal, it is incumbent on management of the organization to prioritize areas of improvement.

### Methodologies

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Methodologies") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section's source code: Methodologies")]

Six Sigma projects follow two project methodologies, inspired by [W. Edwards Deming](/wiki/W._Edwards_Deming "W. Edwards Deming")'s [Plan–Do–Study–Act Cycle](/wiki/PDCA "PDCA"), each with five phases.[[6]](#cite_note-Juran-6)

- DMAIC ("duh-may-ick", /də.ˈmeɪ.ɪk/) is used for projects aimed at improving an existing business process
- DMADV ("duh-mad-vee", /də.ˈmæd.vi/) is used for projects aimed at creating new product or process designs

#### DMAIC

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=5 "Edit section: DMAIC") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section's source code: DMAIC")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/DMAICWebdingsI.png/330px-DMAICWebdingsI.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:DMAICWebdingsI.png)

DMAIC's five steps

Main article: [DMAIC](/wiki/DMAIC "DMAIC")

The DMAIC project methodology has five phases:

- ***D**efine* the system, the voice of the customer and their requirements, and the project goals, specifically.
- ***M**easure* key aspects of the current process and collect relevant data; calculate the "as-is" process capability
- ***A**nalyze* the data to investigate and verify cause and effect. Determine what the relationships are, and attempt to ensure that all factors have been considered. Seek out the root cause of the defect under investigation.
- ***I**mprove* or optimize the current process based upon data analysis using techniques such as [design of experiments](/wiki/Design_of_experiments "Design of experiments"), [poka yoke](/wiki/Poka_yoke "Poka yoke") or mistake proofing, and standard work to create a new, future state process. Set up pilot runs to establish [process capability](/wiki/Process_capability "Process capability").
- ***C**ontrol* the future state process to ensure that any deviations from the target are corrected before they result in defects. Implement [control systems](/wiki/Control_systems "Control systems") such as [statistical process control](/wiki/Statistical_process_control "Statistical process control"), production boards, visual workplaces, and continuously monitor the process. This process is repeated until the desired quality level is obtained.

Some organizations add a ***R**ecognize* step at the beginning, which is to recognize the right problem to work on, thus yielding an RDMAIC methodology.[[12]](#cite_note-WebberWallace2006p43-12)

#### DMADV

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=6 "Edit section: DMADV") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section's source code: DMADV")]

Main article: [Design for Six Sigma](/wiki/Design_for_Six_Sigma "Design for Six Sigma")

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/DMADVWebdingsI.png/500px-DMADVWebdingsI.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:DMADVWebdingsI.png)

DMADV's five steps

Also known as DFSS ("**D**esign **F**or **S**ix **S**igma"), the DMADV methodology's five phases are:[[6]](#cite_note-Juran-6)

- ***D**efine* design goals that are consistent with customer demands and the enterprise strategy.
- ***M**easure* and identify CTQs (characteristics that are **C**ritical **T**o **Q**uality), measure product capabilities, production process capability, and measure risks.
- ***A**nalyze* to develop and design alternatives
- ***D**esign* an improved alternative, best suited per analysis in the previous step
- ***V**erify* the design, set up pilot runs, implement the production process and hand it over to the process owner(s).

### Professionalization

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Professionalization") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section's source code: Professionalization")]

One key innovation of Six Sigma involves professionalizing quality management. Prior to Six Sigma, quality management was largely relegated to the production floor and to [statisticians](/wiki/Statistician "Statistician") in a separate quality department. Formal Six Sigma programs adopt an elite ranking terminology similar to [martial arts](/wiki/Martial_arts "Martial arts") systems like [judo](/wiki/Judo "Judo") to define a hierarchy (and career path) that spans business functions and levels.

Six Sigma identifies several roles for successful implementation:[[13]](#cite_note-mikel-13)

- *Executive Leadership* includes the [CEO](/wiki/CEO "CEO") and other members of top management. They are responsible for setting up a vision for Six Sigma implementation. They also empower other stakeholders with the freedom and resources to transcend departmental barriers and overcome resistance to change.[[14]](#cite_note-14)
- *Champions* take responsibility for Six Sigma implementation across the organization. The Executive Leadership draws them from upper management. Champions also act as mentors to Black Belts.
- *Master Black Belts*, identified by Champions, act as in-house coaches on Six Sigma. They devote all of their time to Six Sigma, assisting Champions and guiding Black Belts and Green Belts. In addition to statistical tasks, they ensure that Six Sigma is applied consistently across departments and job functions.
- *Black Belts* operate under Master Black Belts to apply Six Sigma to specific projects. They also devote all of their time to Six Sigma. They primarily focus on Six Sigma project execution and special leadership with special tasks, whereas Champions and Master Black Belts focus on identifying projects/functions for Six Sigma.
- *Green Belts* are the employees who take up Six Sigma implementation along with their other job responsibilities, operating under the guidance of Black Belts.

According to proponents, special training is needed for all of these practitioners to ensure that they follow the methodology and use the data-driven approach correctly.[[15]](#cite_note-15)

Some organizations use additional belt colors, such as "yellow belts", for employees that have basic training in Six Sigma tools and generally participate in projects, and "white belts" for those locally trained in the concepts but do not participate in the project team. "Orange belts" are also mentioned to be used for special cases.[[16]](#cite_note-HarryMann2011-16)

## Role of the 1.5 sigma shift

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Role of the 1.5 sigma shift") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section's source code: Role of the 1.5 sigma shift")]

Experience has shown that processes usually do not perform as well in the long term as they do in the short term.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9) As a result, the number of sigmas that will fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit may well drop over time, compared to an initial short-term study.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9) To account for this real-life increase in process variation over time, an empirically based 1.5σ shift is introduced into the calculation.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)[[21]](#cite_note-harry-21) Mikel Harry, the creator of Six Sigma, based the 1.5σ shift on the height of a stack of discs. He called this "Benderizing". He claimed that based on his stack, all processes shift 1.5σ every 50 samples. According to this idea, a process that fits 6σ between the process mean and the nearest specification limit in a short-term study will in the long term fit only 4.5σ – either because the process mean will move over time, or because the long-term standard deviation of the process will be greater than that observed in the short term, or both.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)

Hence the widely accepted definition of a 6σ process is a process that produces 3.4 [defective parts per million opportunities](/wiki/Defects_per_million_opportunities "Defects per million opportunities") (DPMO). This is based on the fact that at *each* side of a [normal distribution](/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution"), the fraction of values that lie outside 4.5σ deviation is 3.4/1,000,000, corresponding to the scenario where the limits are 6σ from the "original" mean of zero and the process mean is then shifted by 1.5σ (and therefore, the 6σ limits are no longer symmetrical about the mean). Such a shifted process is commonly referred to as a 4.5σ process.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9) This allows for the fact that special causes may result in a deterioration in process performance over time and is designed to prevent underestimation of the defect levels likely to be encountered in real-life operation.[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)

(The failure rate of a 6σ distribution with the mean shifted 1.5σ is not equivalent to the failure rate of an unshifted "true 4.5σ process" where the limits are 4.5σ away: while the side that the 6σ distribution has moved *towards* would have an elevated proportion of values outside of the limits corresponding to a 4.5σ level, the other side would've seen a drastic decrease of proportions corresponding to a 7.5σ level. In contrast, a true 4.5σ process would have the 4.5σ level on *both* sides, corresponding to a 6.8 DPMO. They would be equivalent if the limit is only present on one side of the distribution.)[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)

The role of the sigma shift is mainly academic. The purpose of 6σ is to generate organizational performance improvement. It is up to the organization to determine, based on customer expectations, what the appropriate sigma level of a process is. The purpose of the sigma value is as a comparative figure to determine whether a process is improving, deteriorating, stagnant or non-competitive with others in the same business. Six Sigma (3.4 DPMO) is not the goal of all processes.

## In practice

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=13 "Edit section: In practice") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section's source code: In practice")]

Six Sigma mostly finds application in large organizations.[[5]](#cite_note-QD-5) According to industry consultants like [Thomas Pyzdek](/wiki/Thomas_Pyzdek "Thomas Pyzdek") and John Kullmann, companies with fewer than 500 employees are less suited to Six Sigma or need to adapt the standard approach to make it work for them.[[5]](#cite_note-QD-5) Six Sigma, however, contains a large number of tools and techniques that work well in small to mid-size organizations. The fact that an organization is not big enough to be able to afford black belts does not diminish its ability to make improvements using this set of tools and techniques. The infrastructure described as necessary to support Six Sigma is a result of the size of the organization rather than a requirement of Six Sigma itself.[[5]](#cite_note-QD-5)

### Manufacturing

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Manufacturing") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section's source code: Manufacturing")]

After its first application at Motorola in the late 1980s, other internationally recognized firms recorded high number of savings after applying Six Sigma. Examples include [Johnson & Johnson](/wiki/Johnson_%26_Johnson "Johnson & Johnson"), with $600 million of reported savings, [Texas Instruments](/wiki/Texas_Instruments "Texas Instruments"), which saved over $500 million as well as [Telefónica](/wiki/Telef%C3%B3nica "Telefónica"), which reported €30 million in savings in the first 10 months; [Sony](/wiki/Sony "Sony") and [Boeing](/wiki/Boeing "Boeing") also reported successfully reducing waste.[[24]](#cite_note-:0-24)

### Engineering and construction

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Engineering and construction") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section's source code: Engineering and construction")]

Although companies have considered common quality control and process improvement strategies, there's still a need for more reasonable and effective methods as all the desired standards and client satisfaction have not always been reached. There is still a need for an essential analysis that can control the factors affecting [concrete](/wiki/Concrete "Concrete") cracks and slippage between concrete and steel. After conducting a case study on Tinjin Xianyi Construction Technology, it was found that construction time and [construction waste](/wiki/Construction_waste "Construction waste") were reduced by 26.2% and 67% accordingly after adopting Six Sigma. Similarly, Six Sigma implementation was studied at one of the largest engineering and construction companies in the world: [Bechtel](/wiki/Bechtel "Bechtel") Corporation, where after an initial investment of $30 million in a Six Sigma program that included identifying and preventing rework and defects, over $200 million were saved.[[24]](#cite_note-:0-24)

### Finance

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Finance") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section's source code: Finance")]

Six Sigma has played an important role by improving the accuracy of allocation of cash to reduce bank charges, automatic payments, improving the accuracy of reporting, reducing documentary credit defects, reducing check collection defects, and reducing variation in collector performance.

For example, [Bank of America](/wiki/Bank_of_America "Bank of America") announced in 2004 that Six Sigma had helped it increase customer satisfaction by 10.4% and decrease customer issues by 24%; similarly, [American Express](/wiki/American_Express "American Express") eliminated non-received renewal credit cards. Other financial institutions that have adopted Six Sigma include [GE Capital](/wiki/GE_Capital "GE Capital") and [JPMorgan Chase](/wiki/JPMorgan_Chase "JPMorgan Chase"), where customer satisfaction was the main objective.[[24]](#cite_note-:0-24)

### Supply chain

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Supply chain") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section's source code: Supply chain")]

In the [supply-chain](/wiki/Supply_chain "Supply chain") field, it is important to ensure that products are delivered to clients at the right time while preserving high-quality standards. By changing the schematic diagram for the supply chain, Six Sigma can ensure quality control on products (defect-free) and guarantee delivery deadlines, the two main issues in the supply chain.[[25]](#cite_note-25)

### Healthcare

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Healthcare") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section's source code: Healthcare")]

This is a sector that has been highly matched with this doctrine for many years because of the nature of zero tolerance for mistakes and potential for reducing medical errors involved in healthcare.[[26]](#cite_note-26)[[27]](#cite_note-27) The goal of Six Sigma in healthcare is broad and includes reducing the inventory of equipment that brings extra costs, altering the process of healthcare delivery in order to make it more efficient and refining reimbursements. A study at the [MD Anderson Cancer Center](/wiki/MD_Anderson_Cancer_Center "MD Anderson Cancer Center"), which recorded an increase in examinations with no additional machines of 45% and a reduction in patients' preparation time of 40 minutes; from 45 minutes to 5 minutes in multiple cases.[[24]](#cite_note-:0-24)

Lean Six Sigma was adopted in 2003 at Stanford hospitals and was introduced at Red Cross hospitals in 2002.[[28]](#cite_note-28)

## Criticism

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Criticism") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section's source code: Criticism")]

While there are many advocates for a Six Sigma approach for the reasons stated above, more than half of projects are unsuccessful: in 2010, the *[Wall Street Journal](/wiki/Wall_Street_Journal "Wall Street Journal")* reported that more than 60% of projects fail.[[29]](#cite_note-29) A review of academic literature found 34 common failure factors in 56 papers on Lean, Six Sigma, and LSS from 1995 to 2013.[[30]](#cite_note-30) Among them are (summarized):

- Lack of top management attitude, commitment, and involvement; lack of leadership and vision
- Lack of training and education; lack of resources (financial, technical, human, etc.)
- Poor project selection and prioritization; weak link to strategic objectives of the organization
- Resistance to culture change; Poor communication; Lack of consideration of the human factors
- Lack of awareness of the benefits of Lean/Six Sigma; Lack of technical understanding of tools, techniques, and practices

Others have provided other criticisms.

### Lack of originality

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Lack of originality") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section's source code: Lack of originality")]

Quality expert [Joseph M. Juran](/wiki/Joseph_M._Juran "Joseph M. Juran") described Six Sigma as "a basic version of quality improvement", stating that "there is nothing new there. It includes what we used to call facilitators. They've adopted more flamboyant terms, like belts with different colors. I think that concept has merit to set apart, to create specialists who can be very helpful. Again, that's not a new idea. The [American Society for Quality](/wiki/American_Society_for_Quality "American Society for Quality") long ago established certificates, such as for [reliability](/wiki/Reliability_engineering "Reliability engineering") engineers."[[31]](#cite_note-paton-31)

### Inadequate for complex manufacturing

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Inadequate for complex manufacturing") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section's source code: Inadequate for complex manufacturing")]

Quality expert [Philip B. Crosby](/wiki/Philip_B._Crosby "Philip B. Crosby") stated that the Six Sigma standard does not go far enough and that customers deserve [defect-free products every time](/wiki/Zero_Defects "Zero Defects").[[32]](#cite_note-QualityAndMe-32) According to Crosby, because the Six Sigma standard allows for 3.4 defects per million opportunities and microchips contain millions of microscopic circuits that must be etched flawlessly, Six Sigma permits all microchips to be defective.[[33]](#cite_note-QualityIsStillFree-33)

### Role of consultants

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Role of consultants") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section's source code: Role of consultants")]

The use of "Black Belts" as itinerant change agents has fostered an industry of training and certification. Critics have argued there is overselling of Six Sigma by too great a number of consulting firms, many of which claim expertise in Six Sigma when they have only a rudimentary understanding of the tools and techniques involved or the markets or industries in which they are acting.[[34]](#cite_note-34)

### Potential negative effects

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Potential negative effects") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section's source code: Potential negative effects")]

A *[Fortune](/wiki/Fortune_(magazine) "Fortune (magazine)")* article stated that "of 58 large companies that have announced Six Sigma programs, 91% have trailed the [S&P 500](/wiki/S%26P_500 "S&P 500") since".[[35]](#cite_note-35) The statement was attributed to "an analysis by Charles Holland of consulting firm [Qualpro](/wiki/Qualpro "Qualpro") (which espouses a competing quality-improvement process)".[[36]](#cite_note-cnn-36) The summary of the article is that Six Sigma is effective at what it is intended to do, but that it is "narrowly designed to fix an existing process" and does not help in "coming up with new products or disruptive technologies."[[37]](#cite_note-wsj-37)[[38]](#cite_note-sbti-38)

### Over-reliance on statistics

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Over-reliance on statistics") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section's source code: Over-reliance on statistics")]

More direct criticism targets the "rigid" nature of Six Sigma, arguing that it has an over-reliance on methods and tools. In most cases, more attention is paid to reducing variation and searching for any significant factors, and less attention is paid to developing robustness in the first place (which can altogether eliminate the need for reducing variation).[[39]](#cite_note-cranfield1-39) The extensive reliance on significance testing and use of multiple regression techniques increase the risk of making commonly unknown types of statistical errors or mistakes. A possible consequence of Six Sigma's array of [*p*-value](/wiki/P-value "P-value") misconceptions is the false belief that the probability of a conclusion being in error can be calculated from the data in a single experiment without reference to external evidence or the plausibility of the underlying mechanism.[[40]](#cite_note-yimg-40) One of the most serious but all-too-common misuses of inferential statistics is to take a model that was developed through exploratory model building and subject it to the same sorts of statistical tests that are used to validate a model that was specified in advance.[[41]](#cite_note-jerry-41)

Another comment refers to the oft-mentioned Transfer Function, which seems to be a flawed theory if looked at in detail.[[42]](#cite_note-ii-42) Since significance tests were first popularized many objections have been voiced by prominent and respected statisticians. The volume of criticism and rebuttal has filled books with language seldom used in the scholarly debate of a dry subject.[[43]](#cite_note-harlow-43)[[44]](#cite_note-morrison-44)[[45]](#cite_note-mccloskey-45)[[46]](#cite_note-chow-46) Much of the first criticism was already published more than 40 years ago (see [Statistical hypothesis testing § Criticism](/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing#Criticism "Statistical hypothesis testing")).

In a 2006 issue of *USA Army Logistician* an article critical of Six Sigma noted: "The dangers of a single paradigmatic orientation (in this case, that of technical rationality) can blind us to values associated with [double-loop learning](/wiki/Double-loop_learning "Double-loop learning") and the [learning organization](/wiki/Learning_organization "Learning organization"), [organization adaptability](/wiki/Adaptability "Adaptability"), workforce creativity and development, humanizing the workplace, [cultural awareness](/wiki/Cultural_competence "Cultural competence"), and strategy making."[[47]](#cite_note-Paparone-47)

### 1.5 sigma shift

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=25 "Edit section: 1.5 sigma shift") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section's source code: 1.5 sigma shift")]

The statistician [Donald J. Wheeler](/wiki/Donald_J._Wheeler "Donald J. Wheeler") has dismissed the 1.5 sigma shift as "goofy" because of its arbitrary nature.[[48]](#cite_note-wheeler-48) Its universal applicability is seen as doubtful.

The 1.5 sigma shift has also become contentious because it results in stated "sigma levels" that reflect short-term rather than long-term performance: a process that has long-term defect levels corresponding to 4.5 sigma performance is, by Six Sigma convention, described as a "six sigma process".[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)[[49]](#cite_note-Pande229-49) The accepted Six Sigma scoring system thus cannot be equated to actual normal distribution probabilities for the stated number of standard deviations, and this has been a key bone of contention over how Six Sigma measures are defined.[[49]](#cite_note-Pande229-49) The fact that it is rarely explained that a "6 sigma" process will have long-term defect rates corresponding to 4.5 sigma performance rather than actual 6 sigma performance has led several commentators to express the opinion that Six Sigma is a [confidence trick](/wiki/Confidence_trick "Confidence trick").[[9]](#cite_note-Tennant-9)

### Stifling creativity in research

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Stifling creativity in research") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section's source code: Stifling creativity in research")]

According to John Dodge, editor in chief of *Design News*, the use of Six Sigma is inappropriate in a research environment. Dodge states, "excessive metrics, steps, measurements and Six Sigma's intense focus on reducing variability water down the discovery process. Under Six Sigma, the free-wheeling nature of brainstorming and the serendipitous side of discovery is stifled."[[50]](#cite_note-dodge-50) He concludes that "there's general agreement that freedom in basic or pure research is preferable while Six Sigma works best in incremental innovation when there's an expressed commercial goal."[[50]](#cite_note-dodge-50)

A *[BusinessWeek](/wiki/BusinessWeek "BusinessWeek")* article says that [James McNerney](/wiki/James_McNerney "James McNerney")'s introduction of Six Sigma at [3M](/wiki/3M "3M") had the effect of stifling creativity and reports its removal from the research function. It cites two [Wharton School](/wiki/Wharton_School_of_the_University_of_Pennsylvania "Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania") professors who say that Six Sigma leads to incremental innovation at the expense of [blue skies research](/wiki/Blue_skies_research "Blue skies research").[[51]](#cite_note-Businessweek-51) This phenomenon is further explored in the book *Going Lean*, which describes a related approach known as [lean dynamics](/wiki/Lean_dynamics "Lean dynamics") and provides data to show that [Ford](/wiki/Ford_Motor_Company "Ford Motor Company")'s 6 Sigma program did little to change its fortunes.[[52]](#cite_note-ruffa-52)

### Lack of documentation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&veaction=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Lack of documentation") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Six_Sigma&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section's source code: Lack of documentation")]

One criticism voiced by Yasar Jarrar and Andy Neely from the [Cranfield School of Management](/wiki/Cranfield_School_of_Management "Cranfield School of Management")'s Centre for Business Performance is that while Six Sigma is a powerful approach, it can also unduly dominate an organization's culture; and they add that much of the Six Sigma literature – in a remarkable way (six-sigma claims to be evidence, scientifically based) – lacks [academic rigor](/wiki/Academic_rigor "Academic rigor"):

.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 32px}.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;margin-top:0}@media(min-width:500px){.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{padding-left:1.6em}}
> One final criticism, probably more to the Six Sigma literature than concepts, relates to the evidence for Six Sigma’s success. So far, documented case studies using the Six Sigma methods are presented as the strongest evidence for its success. However, looking at these documented cases, and apart from a few that are detailed from the experience of leading organizations like GE and Motorola, most cases are not documented in a systemic or academic manner. In fact, the majority are case studies illustrated on websites, and are, at best, sketchy. They provide no mention of any specific Six Sigma methods that were used to resolve the problems. It has been argued that by relying on the Six Sigma criteria, management is lulled into the idea that something is being done about quality, whereas any resulting improvement is accidental (Latzko 1995). Thus, when looking at the evidence put forward for Six Sigma's success, mostly by consultants and people with vested interests, the question that begs to be asked is: are we making a true improvement with Six Sigma methods or just getting skilled at telling stories? Everyone seems to believe that we are making true improvements, but there is some way to go to document these empirically and clarify the causal relations.[[39]](#cite_note-cranfield1-39)
