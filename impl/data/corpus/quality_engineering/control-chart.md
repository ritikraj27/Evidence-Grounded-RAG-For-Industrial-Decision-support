# Control chart

Tool to assess control of a manufacturing process

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Xbar_chart_for_a_paired_xbar_and_R_chart.svg/250px-Xbar_chart_for_a_paired_xbar_and_R_chart.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Xbar_chart_for_a_paired_xbar_and_R_chart.svg)

A control chart

**Control charts** (also known as **Shewhart charts**, after [Walter A. Shewhart](/wiki/Walter_A._Shewhart "Walter A. Shewhart"), or **process-behavior charts**) are [graphical plots](/wiki/Plot_(graphics) "Plot (graphics)") used in [statistical process control](/wiki/Statistical_process_control "Statistical process control") (SPC) to determine whether a [manufacturing](/wiki/Manufacturing_process_management "Manufacturing process management") or [business process](/wiki/Business_process "Business process") is in a state of [statistical control](/wiki/Statistical_control "Statistical control"). They form the basis of modern statistical process control, and are one of the [seven basic tools](/wiki/Seven_basic_tools_of_quality "Seven basic tools of quality") of [quality control](/wiki/Quality_control "Quality control").

A control chart plots a process statistic, such as a [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean"), range, or proportion, over time together with a centre line and upper and lower [control limits](/wiki/Control_limits "Control limits") that distinguish common-cause variation from special-cause variation. Points outside the control limits or non-random patterns within them indicate that the process should be investigated for assignable causes of variation. When a process is in statistical control, control charts can be used to predict its future performance. Control charts were invented by Shewhart at [Bell Labs](/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") in the 1920s.

## Overview

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section's source code: Overview")]

If analysis of the control chart indicates that the process is currently under control (i.e., is stable, with variation only coming from sources common to the process), then no corrections or changes to process control parameters are needed or desired. In addition, data from the process can be used to [predict](/wiki/Predictive_analytics "Predictive analytics") the future performance of the process. If the chart indicates that the monitored process is not in control, analysis of the chart can help determine the sources of [variation](/wiki/Statistical_dispersion "Statistical dispersion"), as this will result in degraded process performance.[[1]](#cite_note-1) A process that is stable but operating outside desired (specification) limits (e.g., scrap rates may be in statistical control but above desired limits) needs to be improved through a deliberate effort to understand the causes of current performance and fundamentally improve the process.[[2]](#cite_note-WheelerUV-2)

The control chart is one of the [seven basic tools](/wiki/Seven_basic_tools_of_quality "Seven basic tools of quality") of [quality control](/wiki/Quality_control "Quality control").[[3]](#cite_note-3) Typically control charts are used for [time-series](/wiki/Time_series "Time series") data, also known as [continuous](/wiki/Continuous_or_discrete_variable "Continuous or discrete variable") data or variable data. Although they can also be used for data that has logical comparability (i.e. you want to compare samples that were taken all at the same time, or the performance of different individuals); however the type of chart used to do this requires consideration.[[4]](#cite_note-4)

## History

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=2 "Edit section: History") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section's source code: History")]

The control chart was invented by [Walter A. Shewhart](/wiki/Walter_A._Shewhart "Walter A. Shewhart") working for [Bell Labs](/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") in the 1920s.[[5]](#cite_note-5) The company's engineers had been seeking to improve the reliability of their [telephony](/wiki/Telephony "Telephony") transmission systems. Because [amplifiers](/wiki/Amplifier "Amplifier") and other equipment had to be buried underground, there was a stronger business need to reduce the frequency of failures and repairs. By 1920, the engineers had already realized the importance of reducing variation in a manufacturing process. Moreover, they had realized that continual process-adjustment in reaction to non-conformance actually increased variation and degraded quality. Shewhart framed the problem in terms of [common- and special-causes](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes") of variation and, on May 16, 1924, wrote an internal memo introducing the control chart as a tool for distinguishing between the two. Shewhart's boss, George Edwards, recalled: "Dr. Shewhart prepared a little memorandum only about a page in length. About a third of that page was given over to a simple diagram which we would all recognize today as a schematic control chart. That diagram, and the short text which preceded and followed it set forth all of the essential principles and considerations which are involved in what we know today as process quality control."[[6]](#cite_note-6) Shewhart stressed that bringing a production process into a state of [statistical control](/wiki/Statistical_control "Statistical control"), where there is only common-cause variation, and keeping it in control, is necessary to predict future output and to manage a process economically.

Shewhart created the basis for the control chart and the concept of a state of statistical control by carefully designed experiments. While Shewhart drew from pure mathematical statistical theories, he understood that data from physical processes typically produce a "[normal distribution](/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution") curve" (a [Gaussian distribution](/wiki/Gaussian_distribution "Gaussian distribution"), also commonly referred to as a "[bell curve](/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution")"). He discovered that observed variation in manufacturing data did not always behave the same way as data in nature ([Brownian motion](/wiki/Brownian_motion "Brownian motion") of particles). Shewhart concluded that while every process displays variation, some processes display controlled variation that is natural to the process, while others display uncontrolled variation that is not present in the process causal system at all times.[[7]](#cite_note-7)

In 1924, or 1925, Shewhart's innovation came to the attention of [W. Edwards Deming](/wiki/W._Edwards_Deming "W. Edwards Deming"), then working at the [Hawthorne facility](/wiki/Hawthorne_facility "Hawthorne facility"). Deming later worked at the [United States Department of Agriculture](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture "United States Department of Agriculture") and became the mathematical advisor to the [United States Census Bureau](/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau "United States Census Bureau"). Over the next half a century, Deming became the foremost champion and proponent of Shewhart's work. After the defeat of [Japan](/wiki/Japan "Japan") at the close of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Deming served as statistical consultant to the [Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers](/wiki/Supreme_Commander_for_the_Allied_Powers "Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers"). His ensuing involvement in Japanese life, and long career as an industrial consultant there, spread Shewhart's thinking, and the use of the control chart, widely in Japanese manufacturing industry throughout the 1950s and 1960s.

Bonnie Small, a high school teacher in Oconomowoc, Wisconsin, read Shewhart's work and was inspired to employ control charts while working at her cousin's photography lab in Chicago. In 1942, she began working for Western Electric at their Hawthorne works in Cicero, her experience at the plant leading her to further refine the use of Shewhart's ideas. When General Electric opened a new plant in Allentown, PA to manufacture the newly developed [transistor](/wiki/Transistor "Transistor"), Small was asked to supervise quality systems at the plant.[[8]](#cite_note-8) She was asked by the company to form a committee for the purpose of codifying her approach to quality and in 1956, the committee published the first edition of *The Western Electric Statistical Quality Control Handbook*.[[9]](#cite_note-Best_&_Neuhauser_2006-9)

## Chart details

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Chart details") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section's source code: Chart details")]

A control chart consists of:

- Points representing a statistic (e.g., a [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean"), range, proportion) of measurements of a quality characteristic in samples taken from the process at different times (i.e., the data)
- The mean of this statistic using all the samples is calculated (e.g., the mean of the means, mean of the ranges, mean of the proportions) - or for a reference period against which change can be assessed. Similarly a median can be used instead.
- A centre line is drawn at the value of the mean or median of the statistic
- The [standard deviation](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") (e.g., sqrt(variance) of the mean) of the statistic is calculated using all the samples - or again for a reference period against which change can be assessed. in the case of XmR charts, strictly it is an approximation of standard deviation, the [*[clarification needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] does not make the assumption of homogeneity of process over time that the standard deviation makes.
- Upper and lower [control limits](/wiki/Control_limits "Control limits") (sometimes called "natural process limits") that indicate the threshold at which the process output is considered statistically 'unlikely' and are drawn typically at 3 standard deviations from the center line

The chart may have other optional features, including:

- More restrictive upper and lower warning or control limits, drawn as separate lines, typically two standard deviations above and below the center line. This is regularly used when a process needs tighter controls on variability.
- Division into zones, with the addition of rules governing frequencies of observations in each zone
- Annotation with events of interest, as determined by the Quality Engineer in charge of the process' quality
- Action on special causes

(n.b., there are several rule sets for detection of signal; this is just one set. The rule set should be clearly stated.)

1. Any point outside the control limits
2. A Run of 7 Points all above or all below the central line - Stop the production
   - Quarantine and 100% check
   - Adjust Process.
   - Check 5 Consecutive samples
   - Continue The Process.
3. A Run of 7 Point Up or Down - Instruction as above

### Control chart constant

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Control chart constant") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section's source code: Control chart constant")]

The *control chart constant* or *bias correction factor* are [constants](/wiki/Constant_(mathematics) "Constant (mathematics)") used in control charts.[[10]](#cite_note-Aft-10) The constants are based on the subgroup size (n) and are derived from the statistical properties of sampling distributions.[[11]](#cite_note-11)[[12]](#cite_note-12) The first control chart constants proposed were *d*2 and *d*3.[[13]](#cite_note-Kaczynski-13)

### Chart usage

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Chart usage") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section's source code: Chart usage")]

If the process is in control (and the process statistic is normal), 99.7300% of all the points will fall between the control limits. Any observations outside the limits, or systematic patterns within, suggest the introduction of a new (and likely unanticipated) source of variation, known as a [special-cause](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes") variation. Since increased variation means increased [quality costs](/wiki/Quality_costs "Quality costs"), a control chart "signaling" the presence of a special-cause requires immediate investigation.

This makes the control limits very important decision aids. The control limits provide information about the process behavior and have no intrinsic relationship to any [specification](/wiki/Specification "Specification") targets or [engineering tolerance](/wiki/Engineering_tolerance "Engineering tolerance"). In practice, the process mean (and hence the centre line) may not coincide with the specified value (or target) of the quality characteristic because the process design simply cannot deliver the process characteristic at the desired level.

Control charts limit [specification limits](/wiki/Specification_(technical_standard) "Specification (technical standard)") or targets because of the tendency of those involved with the process (e.g., machine operators) to focus on performing to specification when in fact the least-cost course of action is to keep process variation as low as possible. Attempting to make a process whose natural centre is not the same as the target perform to target specification increases process variability and increases costs significantly and is the cause of much inefficiency in operations. [Process capability](/wiki/Process_capability "Process capability") studies do examine the relationship between the natural process limits (the control limits) and specifications, however.

The purpose of control charts is to allow simple detection of events that are indicative of an increase in process variability.[[14]](#cite_note-14) This simple decision can be difficult where the process characteristic is continuously varying; the control chart provides statistically objective criteria of change. When change is detected and considered good its cause should be identified and possibly become the new way of working, where the change is bad then its cause should be identified and eliminated.

The purpose in adding warning limits or subdividing the control chart into zones is to provide early notification if something is amiss. Instead of immediately launching a process improvement effort to determine whether special causes are present, the Quality Engineer may temporarily increase the rate at which samples are taken from the process output until it is clear that the process is truly in control. Note that with three-sigma limits, [common-cause](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes") variations result in signals less than once out of every twenty-two points for skewed processes and about once out of every three hundred seventy (1/370.4) points for normally distributed processes.[[15]](#cite_note-Wheeler112010-15) The two-sigma warning levels will be reached about once for every twenty-two (1/21.98) plotted points in normally distributed data. (For example, the means of sufficiently large samples drawn from practically any underlying distribution whose variance exists are normally distributed, according to the Central Limit Theorem.)

### Choice of limits

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Choice of limits") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section's source code: Choice of limits")]

Shewhart set *3-sigma* (3-standard deviation) limits on the following basis.

- The coarse result of [Chebyshev's inequality](/wiki/Chebyshev%27s_inequality "Chebyshev's inequality") that, for any [probability distribution](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"), the [probability](/wiki/Probability "Probability") of an outcome greater than *k* [standard deviations](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") from the [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean") is at most 1/*k*2.
- The finer result of the [Vysochanskii–Petunin inequality](/wiki/Vysochanskii%E2%80%93Petunin_inequality "Vysochanskii–Petunin inequality"), that for any [unimodal probability distribution](/wiki/Unimodal_probability_distribution "Unimodal probability distribution"), the [probability](/wiki/Probability "Probability") of an outcome greater than *k* [standard deviations](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") from the [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean") is at most 4/(9*k*2).
- In the [Normal distribution](/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution"), a very common [probability distribution](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"), 99.7% of the observations occur within three [standard deviations](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") of the [mean](/wiki/Mean "Mean") (see [Normal distribution](/wiki/Normal_distribution#Standard_deviation_and_coverage "Normal distribution")).

Shewhart summarized the conclusions by saying:

> *... the fact that the criterion which we happen to use has a fine ancestry in highbrow statistical theorems does not justify its use. Such justification must come from empirical evidence that it works. As the practical engineer might say, the proof of the pudding is in the eating.*[[16]](#cite_note-16)

Although he initially experimented with limits based on [probability distributions](/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"), Shewhart ultimately wrote:

> *Some of the earliest attempts to characterize a state of statistical control were inspired by the belief that there existed a special form of frequency function* f *and it was early argued that the normal law characterized such a state. When the normal law was found to be inadequate, then generalized functional forms were tried. Today, however, all hopes of finding a unique functional form* f *are blasted.*[[17]](#cite_note-17)

The control chart is intended as a [heuristic](/wiki/Heuristic "Heuristic"). [Deming](/wiki/W._Edwards_Deming "W. Edwards Deming") insisted that it is not a [hypothesis test](/wiki/Hypothesis_test "Hypothesis test") and is not motivated by the [Neyman–Pearson lemma](/wiki/Neyman%E2%80%93Pearson_lemma "Neyman–Pearson lemma"). He contended that the disjoint nature of [population](/wiki/Population_(statistics) "Population (statistics)") and [sampling frame](/wiki/Sampling_frame "Sampling frame") in most industrial situations compromised the use of conventional statistical techniques. [Deming](/wiki/W._Edwards_Deming "W. Edwards Deming")'s intention was to seek insights into the [cause system](/wiki/Cause_system "Cause system") of a process *...under a wide range of unknowable circumstances, future and past....*[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] He claimed that, under such conditions, *3-sigma* limits provided *... a rational and economic guide to minimum economic loss...* from the two errors:[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

1. *Ascribe a variation or a mistake to a special cause (assignable cause) when in fact the cause belongs to the system (common cause).* (Also known as a [Type I error](/wiki/Type_I_and_type_II_errors "Type I and type II errors") or False Positive)
2. *Ascribe a variation or a mistake to the system (common causes) when in fact the cause was a special cause (assignable cause).* (Also known as a [Type II error](/wiki/Type_I_and_type_II_errors "Type I and type II errors") or False Negative)

### Calculation of standard deviation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Calculation of standard deviation") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section's source code: Calculation of standard deviation")]

As for the calculation of control limits, the [standard deviation](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") (error) required is that of the [common-cause](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes") variation in the process. Hence, the usual [estimator](/wiki/Estimator "Estimator"), in terms of sample variance, is not used as this estimates the total squared-error loss from both [common- and special-causes](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes") of variation.

An alternative method is to use the relationship between the [range](/wiki/Range_(statistics) "Range (statistics)") of a sample and its [standard deviation](/wiki/Standard_deviation "Standard deviation") derived by [Leonard H. C. Tippett](/wiki/Leonard_Henry_Caleb_Tippett "Leonard Henry Caleb Tippett"), as an estimator which tends to be less influenced by the extreme observations which typify [special-causes](/wiki/Common-_and_special-causes "Common- and special-causes").[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Rules for detecting signals

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Rules for detecting signals") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section's source code: Rules for detecting signals")]

The most common sets are:

- The [Western Electric rules](/wiki/Western_Electric_rules "Western Electric rules")
- The [Wheeler](/wiki/Donald_J._Wheeler "Donald J. Wheeler") rules (equivalent to the Western Electric zone tests[[18]](#cite_note-18))
- The [Nelson rules](/wiki/Nelson_rules "Nelson rules")

There has been particular controversy as to how long a run of observations, all on the same side of the centre line, should count as a signal, with 6, 7, 8 and 9 all being advocated by various writers.

The most important principle for choosing a set of rules is that the choice be made before the data is inspected. Choosing rules once the data have been seen tends to increase the [Type I error](/wiki/Type_I_error "Type I error") rate owing to [testing effects suggested by the data](/wiki/Testing_hypotheses_suggested_by_the_data "Testing hypotheses suggested by the data").

## Alternative bases

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Alternative bases") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section's source code: Alternative bases")]

In 1935, the [British Standards Institution](/wiki/British_Standard "British Standard"), under the influence of [Egon Pearson](/wiki/Egon_Pearson "Egon Pearson") and against Shewhart's spirit, adopted control charts, replacing *3-sigma* limits with limits based on [percentiles](/wiki/Percentile "Percentile") of the [normal distribution](/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution"). This move continues to be represented by [John Oakland](/w/index.php?title=John_Oakland&action=edit&redlink=1 "John Oakland (page does not exist)") and others but has been widely deprecated by writers in the Shewhart–Deming tradition.

## Performance of control charts

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Performance of control charts") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section's source code: Performance of control charts")]

When a point falls outside the limits established for a given control chart, those responsible for the underlying process are expected to determine whether a special cause has occurred. If one has, it is appropriate to determine if the results with the special cause are better than or worse than results from common causes alone. If worse, then that cause should be eliminated if possible. If better, it may be appropriate to intentionally retain the special cause within the system producing the results.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Even when a process is *in control* (that is, no special causes are present in the system), there is approximately a 0.27% probability of a point exceeding *3-sigma* control limits. So, even an in control process plotted on a properly constructed control chart will eventually signal the possible presence of a special cause, even though one may not have actually occurred. For a Shewhart control chart using *3-sigma* limits, this *false alarm* occurs on average once every 1/0.0027 or 370.4 observations. Therefore, the *in-control average run length* (or in-control ARL) of a Shewhart chart is 370.4.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Meanwhile, if a special cause does occur, it may not be of sufficient magnitude for the chart to produce an immediate *alarm condition*. If a special cause occurs, one can describe that cause by measuring the change in the mean and/or variance of the process in question. When those changes are quantified, it is possible to determine the out-of-control ARL for the chart.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

It turns out that Shewhart charts are quite good at detecting large changes in the process mean or variance, as their out-of-control ARLs are fairly short in these cases. However, for smaller changes (such as a *1-* or *2-sigma* change in the mean), the Shewhart chart does not detect these changes efficiently. Other types of control charts have been developed, such as the [EWMA chart](/wiki/EWMA_chart "EWMA chart"), the [CUSUM](/wiki/CUSUM "CUSUM") chart and the real-time contrasts chart, which detect smaller changes more efficiently by making use of information from observations collected prior to the most recent data point.[[19]](#cite_note-Deng2012-19)

Many control charts work best for numeric data with Gaussian assumptions. The real-time contrasts chart was proposed to monitor process with complex characteristics, e.g. high-dimensional, mix numerical and categorical, missing-valued, non-Gaussian, non-linear relationship.[[19]](#cite_note-Deng2012-19)

## Criticisms

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&veaction=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Criticisms") | [edit source](/w/index.php?title=Control_chart&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section's source code: Criticisms")]

Several authors have criticised the control chart on the grounds that it violates the [likelihood principle](/wiki/Likelihood_principle "Likelihood principle").[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] However, the principle is itself controversial and supporters of control charts further argue that, in general, it is impossible to specify a [likelihood function](/wiki/Likelihood_function "Likelihood function") for a process not in statistical control, especially where knowledge about the [cause system](/wiki/Cause_system "Cause system") of the process is weak.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Some authors have criticised the use of average run lengths (ARLs) for comparing control chart performance, because that average usually follows a [geometric distribution](/wiki/Geometric_distribution "Geometric distribution"), which has high variability and difficulties.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Some authors have criticized that most control charts focus on numeric data. Nowadays, process data can be much more complex, e.g. non-Gaussian, mix numerical and categorical, or be missing-valued.[[19]](#cite_note-Deng2012-19)

†Some practitioners also recommend the use of Individuals charts for attribute data, particularly when the assumptions of either binomially distributed data (p- and np-charts) or Poisson-distributed data (u- and c-charts) are violated.[[23]](#cite_note-Wheeler2000-23) Two primary justifications are given for this practice. First, normality is not necessary for statistical control, so the Individuals chart may be used with non-normal data.[[24]](#cite_note-Staufer2010a-24) Second, attribute charts derive the measure of dispersion directly from the mean proportion (by assuming a probability distribution), while Individuals charts derive the measure of dispersion from the data, independent of the mean, making Individuals charts more robust than attributes charts to violations of the assumptions about the distribution of the underlying population.[[25]](#cite_note-WheelerSPC-25) It is sometimes noted that the substitution of the Individuals chart works best for large counts, when the binomial and [Poisson distributions](/wiki/Poisson_distribution "Poisson distribution") approximate a normal distribution. i.e. when the number of trials n > 1000 for p- and np-charts or λ > 500 for u- and c-charts.

Critics of this approach argue that control charts should not be used when their underlying assumptions are violated, such as when process data is neither normally distributed nor binomially (or Poisson) distributed. Such processes are not in control and should be improved before the application of control charts. Additionally, application of the charts in the presence of such deviations increases the [type I and type II error](/wiki/Type_I_and_type_II_errors "Type I and type II errors") rates of the control charts, and may make the chart of little practical use.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
