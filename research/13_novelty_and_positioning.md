**The verification rule.** Every identifier printed here was resolved to a live record on 11 November 2026 and its title confirmed by reading that record — the arXiv API entry (title, authors, date, `comment` field), the ACL Anthology `.bib` record (title, authors, venue, pages, DOI), or the Crossref / OpenAlex record (title, venue, year, DOI). Nothing is cited from memory. Where a candidate identifier resolved to a *different* paper it was discarded rather than "corrected"; §10 lists what was discarded and what could not be resolved at all.

**What a negative result means here.** A null result means **"not found by this search"**, not "does not exist". **The three distinctions kept throughout.** (a) a paper that **reports the same number**; (b) a paper that **makes the same claim**; (c) a paper that **occupies the position**. A finding can be (a) and still worth reporting; a thesis can lose novelty to (c) while keeping its measurement.

## 2. Finding 1 — Rubric design dominates reader specialisation in LLM annotator panels

**The claim, as the thesis would publish it.** With the pairs, the detector, the regime assignment and the reader model all held constant, **rewriting the annotation rubric moved the measured detection precision of the same detector by a factor of nine** (0.075 [0.026, 0.199] → 0.675 [0.520, 0.799] under the shipped profile; 0.098 [0.039, 0.225] → 0.683 [0.530, 0.804] under the regulatory profile), while **moving the reader from a generalist to a domain specialist moved it by 0.049 under the old rubric and 0.000 under the new one**. Domain-specialised LLM annotator panels therefore buy **agreement, not accuracy** — unless the rubric is specified first.

**The thesis's own numbers** (`impl/results_industrial/exp14_revision_eval.json`, `exp21_rubric_factorial.json`; 240 pairs, 41 alarms per cell):

| Cell | Rubric | Reader | Consensus detection precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| generalist_v1 | v1 | generalist | 0.293 [0.176, 0.445] |
| generalist_v2 | v2 | generalist | **0.659** [0.505, 0.784] |
| specialist_v1 | v1 | specialist ×4 (one per regime) | 0.341 [0.216, 0.495] |
| specialist_v2 | v2 | specialist ×4 | **0.659** [0.505, 0.784] |

Effects in the same units: rubric **+0.366** at the generalist cell, **+0.317** at the specialist cell; specialisation **+0.049** at v1, **0.000** at v2; interaction 0.049. Agreement moves the other way — `generalist_control` (new rubric) against each regime specialist: κ = 0.724 / 0.686 / 0.661 / 0.651 (n = 44 / 52 / 133 / 11); `judge_a_model` (old rubric) against the same four: κ = **0.436 / 0.204 / 0.146 / 0.074**.

**What the literature already establishes.** (i) *The rubric/guideline is a first-class error source in LLM annotation — established.* `arXiv:2412.14461` (*To Err Is Human; To Annotate, SILICON?*) decomposes LLM-annotation measurement error into four sources, the first being *"guideline-induced error from inconsistent annotation criteria"*, alongside *"prompt-induced error from suboptimal meta-instruction"* and *"model-induced error from architectural differences across LLMs"* — rubric, prompt and model identity are already separated, and model identity is already framed as an error to control rather than a capability to buy. `arXiv:2605.20809` (*Refining and Reusing Annotation Guidelines for LLM Annotation*, **ACL 2026 Main**) states the positive form — LLMs "often struggle with the specialized conventions of gold-standard benchmarks", and guideline refinement is proposed "as an alignment mechanism", with "the efficacy of guideline integration" as hypothesis 1. `arXiv:2510.12835` (*Repurposing Annotation Guidelines to Instruct LLM Annotators: A Case Study*, **NLDB 2025**) reports the same in the medical case study. (ii) *Prompt wording is a measurable factor comparable to which model does the reading — established, in the same design shape.* `arXiv:2611.10048` (*Veterans' Mental Health Crisis Severity Annotation*, **IEEE Big Data 2026**) evaluates 15 open-source and 10 proprietary LLMs "across eight prompt variants", examining "annotation performance, inter-model agreement, **prompt sensitivity**", and finds the LLMs "demonstrated substantially higher inter-model consistency, with ordinal agreement (α) reaching 0.8336 under the baseline prompt" against human experts at α = 0.494. (iii) *Panels of LLM annotators, and comparisons of annotator source, are established.* `arXiv:2605.03624` (*Annotation Quality in Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis: A Case Study Comparing Experts, Students, Crowdworkers, …* — the title continues beyond what the arXiv listing showed; the paper compares four annotation sources, students, crowdworkers, LLMs and experts, against an expert ground truth by inter-annotator agreement and downstream performance); on ACL 2026, `2026.findings-acl.4` (*Large Language Models Are Effective Human Annotation Assistants, But Not Good Independent Annotators*), `2026.findings-acl.599` (*A Herd of Language Models Makes a Better Zero-shot Annotator for Clinical Named Entity Recognition* — the panel-as-annotator practice, published), `2026.emnlp-main.2037` (*Systematic Failure Analysis of Open-Weight LLM Annotators*), `2026.findings-acl.2124` (*From Fallback to Frontline: When Can LLMs be Superior Annotators of Human Perspectives?*); also `arXiv:2503.13657` (*Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?*), whose MAST taxonomy is the closest published account of *why* LLM panels fail (150 traces, κ = 0.88). Closest on the rubric side: `arXiv:2606.29920` / ACL 2026 Main `2026.emnlp-main.2192` (*Can LLM-as-a-Judge Reliably Verify Rubrics in Agentic Scenarios?*), whose RuVerBench pairs 2,458 outputs with a rubric and a human label and finds "even the most advanced models achieve strong performance but still exhibit substantial noise".

**Nearest prior work, and the verdict.** Nearest is `arXiv:2412.14461` (SILICON), with `arXiv:2605.20809` and `arXiv:2611.10048` as the two closest supporting positions. *Same number?* No — no paper located reports a nine-fold movement of a detector's measured precision attributable to a rubric rewrite. *Same claim?* **Partly, and this must be conceded** — that guideline wording dominates model identity as a source of annotation variation is published, so "how the task is specified matters more than who reads it" is, in qualitative form, **already known**. *Occupies the position?* No — SILICON is a framework for management research, `2605.20809` is about aligning LLMs to gold conventions, `2611.10048` is about crisis-severity annotation; none reports a **measurement of a system** (the detector's own precision) as the outcome variable of a rubric manipulation. **Verdict: a sharper version of an existing result.** The novelty that survives is about *where* the rubric effect lands, not whether it exists.

**Residual novelty, in one sentence.** The published literature treats rubric sensitivity as a property of **the labels**; the thesis shows it is a property of **the numbers you report about your own system** — a rubric rewrite changed the measured detection precision of an unchanged detector by a factor of nine, so "LLM annotators are guideline-sensitive" propagates straight into evaluation validity.

**The strongest reviewer objection, and whether the evidence answers it.** *"Your 'generalist' and your 'specialist' are the same base model with a different brief. You have measured brief quality, not domain expertise, and relabelled it specialisation. And the factor of nine rests on three alarms: 3 of 40 against 27 of 40."* — **Answered in part, and the thesis must say so in this form.** The design *is* a factorial with the pairs, the detector and its configuration, the regime assignment and the reader model held constant, and there is a model-matched cell (occupational safety, same reader model, rubric v1 against a v2 replicate: 0.083 → 0.167 on 12 alarms over 133 pairs) in which the rubric moves the number with specialisation held fixed; the two effects are estimated separately in the same units (rubric +0.366 / +0.317 against specialisation +0.049 / 0.000) and the CIs on the two detection precisions do not overlap. What the evidence does **not** answer is the substance of the objection: because the reader model is held constant *by design*, "specialisation" here is **specialisation as delivered by a brief**, not as a capability. The finding must therefore be published as *"the specification of the task moves the measurement more than the identity of the reader does"* — better evidence for the practice than the overreaching version. The small-base objection is answered by the non-overlapping intervals but not by the magnitude: with 41 alarms the *direction* is solid and the *size* of the factor is an estimate with wide error.

## 3. Finding 2 — A conflict detector's precision decomposes into detection and typing

**The claim, as the thesis would publish it.** A contradiction detector's reported precision conflates two questions — *is there a conflict here?* (detection) and *is the branch that raised the alarm the branch that owns the conflict?* (typing) — and they can be nine-fold apart. On 240 annotated regulatory pairs the thesis's joint NLI-and-topical gate scores **0.659 [0.505, 0.784] on detection and 0.073 [0.025, 0.194] on typing**. The detector finds the conflicts and reports the wrong kind: it calls supersessions contradictions, because nothing in the retrieved passages says which issue either of them is.

**The thesis's own numbers** (`exp14_revision_eval.json`, `exp20_quantity_stratum.json`, `exp20_quantity_stratum_specialist_labels.json`):

| Profile | Detection precision | Typing precision | n flagged | n pairs | n annotated conflicts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| regulatory | 0.659 [0.505, 0.784] | **0.073** [0.025, 0.194] | 41 | 240 | 84 |
| shipped | 0.650 [0.495, 0.779] | **0.000** [0.000, 0.088] | 40 | 240 | 84 |

Branch decomposition (regulatory): the semantic branch flagged 38 pairs at detection 0.632 / typing 0.000; the revision branch flagged 3 pairs at detection 1.000 / typing 1.000 — so the only typing successes are the three revision-branch alarms, and the supersessions that dominate the corpus are typed as contradictions. The quantity branch fails the other way: across **1,776 cross-document quantity-conflict candidates** (64 measurement terms, exhaustive over the corpus) it fired on **13** of 119 scored pairs; on the **35 construction-labelled limit changes** its recall was **0.000**; and a blind regime specialist confirmed **0 of the 60 pairs it was shown** (58 `term_mismatch`, 2 `no_conflict`).

**What the literature already establishes.** (i) *That conflict detection is not conflict typing — established.* `arXiv:2506.08500` (*DRAGged into Conflicts*) "first propose a novel taxonomy of knowledge conflict types in RAG, along with the desired model behavior for each type", and introduces **CONFLICTS**, "a high-quality benchmark with expert annotations of conflict types in a realistic RAG setting". `arXiv:2607.25959` (Kontrast) introduces "a taxonomy of cross-modal knowledge inconsistencies, covering information granularity differences, direct conflicts, temporal changes, and KG incompleteness" — the same distinction the thesis's branch structure makes, and the reason it has one. `arXiv:2605.27157` (*Detecting Is Not Resolving*) states the general form over 50,000+ turn-level evaluations across four model families: models "exhibit a monitoring-control gap: they readily acknowledge contradictory evidence, yet this awareness fails to constrain their final recommendations — detecting epistemic conflict does not imply resolving it safely". (ii) *That typing a conflict is hard even for frontier models — established.* `arXiv:2610.36915` (*Do We Need an LLM to Know Why Sources Conflict?*) reports that "telling a generator the conflict type improves its responses by about 24 points, but that **predicting the type is hard even for frontier LLMs**", and measures typing accuracy directly on CONFLICTS: decision models 50.0–67.0%, best LLM (Gemini 3.8 Flash) 70.0%; "decomposing the taxonomy into yes/no questions lowers accuracy for every model, and a repair fitted on one model does not transfer". (iii) *That domain-specific, expert-grounded conflict classification benchmarks exist — established.* `arXiv:2608.28607` (RegDivergence-101) is a "101-pair expert-grounded pilot evaluation benchmark" whose labels are "grounded in three peer-reviewed FDA/EMA comparison studies and primary FDA [sources]"; `arXiv:2510.03418` (LegalWiz) "injects six structured contradiction types, and models both self- and pairwise inconsistencies" into synthetic legal documents with "human-in-the-loop validation"; `arXiv:2403.14258` (contradiction extraction from patents), `arXiv:2311.09182` (ContraDoc, **NAACL 2024**) and `arXiv:2604.25313` (Faithfulness-QA) complete the neighbourhood.

**Nearest prior work, and the verdict.** Nearest is `arXiv:2506.08500` (CONFLICTS) for the taxonomy and benchmark, `arXiv:2610.36915` for the difficulty of typing, `arXiv:2605.27157` for the detection-does-not-imply-use principle. *Same number?* No — no paper located reports a single detector's precision split into a detection figure and a typing figure. *Same claim?* **No — this is where the finding stands.** The published claim is "typing is hard" and "conflicts must be typed"; the thesis's claim is a *measurement claim about one detector*, that its two sub-questions are nine-fold apart on the same alarms, so a single "precision" number for a conflict detector is a compound statistic that hides the failure. CONFLICTS makes typing measurable; it does not report the **detection-versus-typing precision gap of an operating detector**, nor the pairing "high detection, low typing". *Occupies the position?* Partly — `2605.27157` occupies detection-versus-use at the level of model behaviour, the thesis detection-versus-typing at the level of a detector's reported precision; adjacent, not the same slot. **Verdict: new as a measurement, and a boundary condition on an existing principle.**

**Residual novelty, in one sentence.** Reporting a conflict detector's precision as one number conceals a nine-fold gap between its two sub-questions, so the thesis contributes the **decomposition itself as an evaluation metric** — detection precision and typing precision reported separately, with the typing branch additionally shown to fire only where its construction guarantees success and to have recall **0.000** on the stratum where numeric conflict provably exists.

**The strongest reviewer objection, and whether the evidence answers it.** *"Typing requires knowing which issue of a regulation is in force. You are penalising a detector for information the retrieved passages do not contain. That is a corpus limitation, not a finding about detectors."* — **Answered, and the answer is in the thesis's own favour.** The typing precision is 0.073 *because* the passages carry no issue metadata — and that is the point: a detector that raises supersessions as contradictions issues a **false** alarm to a practitioner, whatever the reason it could not have known. The stronger counter-argument is that the detection is real and large: the semantic branch flagged 38 pairs of which **24 carried the `supersession` label** at detection 0.632, and on the environmental-control stratum alone the specialist read 25 flags at detection **0.880 [0.700, 0.958]** with recall **0.710** over 31 annotated conflicts. So the detector finds the conflicts; what it cannot do is say which kind — and that failure is precisely the signal that it is being asked a question its inputs cannot answer. That converts the negative into a specification: a detector for regulatory text must be given the version metadata, or must be scoped to detection only. The quantity branch's recall of **0.000** on 35 construction-labelled limit changes is the crisper of the two findings and should be reported as such.

## 4. Finding 3 — A per-claim confidence band is a filter, not a ranking

**The claim, as the thesis would publish it.** Keeping only the claims that a per-claim entailment band tags `grounded` raises the share of answers containing no wrong claim from **34.4% to 50.0%** on the gold protocol and **59.4% to 81.2%** on the retrieved protocol, at the cost of keeping **41.5%** of claims — so the band is a usable **filter**. It is not a usable **ranking**: at the same claim budget, keeping the **first** claims of an answer keeps fewer wrong claims than the band does (paired Wilcoxon **p = 0.008**, d_z = 0.465; n = 30 answers, 345 claims), and the position advantage is not a length artefact (Spearman position-vs-length −0.195, p = 0.0003). A confidence mechanism that a positional heuristic beats is not a confidence mechanism to act on.

**The thesis's own numbers** (`impl/results_focus/exp17_claim_band_eval.json` (gold) and `exp18_claim_band_eval.json` (retrieved); 350 claims, 347 labelled, 32 answers):

| | Gold protocol | Retrieved protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Answers with no wrong claim, unfiltered | 0.344 (11 / 32) | 0.594 (19 / 32) |
| Answers with no wrong claim, `grounded` only | **0.500** (16 / 32) | **0.813** (26 / 32) |
| Claims retained under the `grounded` rule | 0.415 | 0.415 |
| Band vs independent reference, three-class κ | 0.056 [−0.009, 0.121] | 0.045 [−0.021, 0.112] |
| Band vs independent reference, binary κ | 0.049 [−0.026, 0.124] | 0.069 [−0.002, 0.139] |
| Per-band error rate: grounded / inferred / uncertain | 0.542 / 0.513 / 0.610 | 0.118 / 0.103 / 0.220 |

Two readings, both needed. On the evidence the band itself saw (retrieved), the three bands **are** ordered — 0.118 against 0.220 — and the filtering gain is large. On an **independent** reference (gold), the bands are **indistinguishable** (0.542 / 0.513 / 0.610) and κ is indistinguishable from zero. The industrial replication on a different generator is weaker still: κ = −0.040 [−0.159, 0.079] (n = 73), and the band loses every paired comparison, including to the positional baseline (p = 0.500 on 12 answers).

**What the literature already establishes.** (i) *Per-claim confidence labelling with a published statistical procedure is established, and the thesis's three-band labelling is a coarser variant of it.* `arXiv:2610.34872` (*Adaptive Retrieval, Selective Answers: Evaluating Conformal Factuality in Agentic RAG*) evaluates claim-level conformal factuality across six model-dataset configurations, reporting at the 95% target "non-empty response coverage ranges 17.23%–60.17%, claim retention 10.72%–43.16%, and full support among non-empty outputs 89.14%–95.56%". `arXiv:2609.38222` (*Conformal Factuality Control for Multi-Hop RAG*) is closest to the thesis's own filter result, in the same shape: "increasingly stringent conformal targets consistently increase the fraction of responses whose retained claims are fully supported. At the 95% target, this rate ranges from 95.80% to 97.20%, compared with 55.60%–76.03% without filtering. However, the improvement is strongly selective: only 4.41%–31.09%" of claims are retained. On the three-band taxonomy itself, `arXiv:2609.04442` (GRACE) classifies claims as Grounded / Refuted / Boundary by weighted graph centrality, and `arXiv:2305.14627` (ALCE) owns the underlying NLI-based citation recall / precision metric family. (ii) *That the usefulness of claim filtering is contested, and that informativeness-aware metrics are needed — established.* `arXiv:2603.16817` (*Is Conformal Factuality for RAG-based LLMs Robust?*, 56 pages) analyses conformal factuality "across generation, scoring, calibration, robustness, and efficiency", proposes "novel informativeness-aware metrics that better reflect task utility under conformal filtering", and reports that "conformal filtering suffers from low usefulness at hi[gh coverage]". `arXiv:2505.17126` (*Conformal Language Model Reasoning with Coherent Factuality*) makes the neighbouring point sharply: claim-level conformal filtering "can be effective for tasks such as information retrieval, where constituent claims may be evaluated in isolation for factuality, but is not appropriate for reasoning tasks, as steps of a logical argument can be evaluated for correctness only within the context of the claims that precede them" — a claim-level confidence that ignores position and context is mis-specified for anything ordered. (iii) *That per-claim auditing of answers is a live, benchmarked line — established.* `arXiv:2609.10293` (GANDR) pairs a Drafter with a separate Critic that "audits each claim against its cited source and emits a per-claim audit trace on every round" under "a strict correctness criterion requiring every citation to resolve to a passage the retriever returned", reaching 70.8% strict accuracy on a 185-item legal benchmark; `arXiv:2503.10702` (ClaimTrust) propagates trust across claims via a PageRank-like graph.

**Nearest prior work, and the verdict.** Nearest is `arXiv:2609.38222` for the filtering result, `arXiv:2603.16817` for the critique of that result's usefulness, `arXiv:2610.34872` for the mechanism, `arXiv:2505.17126` for the ordering argument. *Same number?* **Yes, in shape** — `2609.38222` reports exactly the pattern the thesis reports, filtering claims raising the share of clean answers substantially at a heavy retention cost, and the thesis's numbers (34.4% → 50.0% at 41.5% kept) sit in the same region as the published 55.6%–76.0% → 95.8%–97.2% at 4.4%–31.1% retention; this is category (a). *Same claim?* **No** — no paper located reports that a **positional heuristic outperforms a confidence-based claim selector**; the published claims concern coverage guarantees, calibration and informativeness, not a trivial position baseline beating the mechanism, and searches for that comparison (claim selection against position baselines; "first claims" and "lead sentence" attribution baselines; heuristic baselines in attribution evaluation) returned nothing in the spaces searched. *Occupies the position?* Partly — `2603.16817` occupies "the filtering result needs an informativeness-aware metric" and `2505.17126` occupies "claim-level filtering is mis-specified where order carries meaning"; neither occupies "the mechanism was beaten by a positional baseline, therefore it should not be acted on". **Verdict: new as a measurement.** The filter result is published (in shape and, for conformal variants, in stronger numbers); the *ranking* result is not, and it is the finding that changes what a practitioner should do with the mechanism.

**Residual novelty, in one sentence.** The thesis contributes the **first comparison of a per-claim confidence mechanism against a positional baseline under a matched claim budget**, plus the observation that the same band is ordered on the evidence it saw (0.118 / 0.103 / 0.220) and *indistinguishable* on an independent reference (0.542 / 0.513 / 0.610) — that is, the band measures agreement with itself, not correctness.

**The strongest reviewer objection, and whether the evidence answers it.** *"Your positional baseline is a property of how your generator writes: it puts the summary first. You have shown that a first-claim prior beats your NLI band, which shows that NLI bands are bad at first-claim summarisation. That is a finding about your corpus's answer shape, not about confidence mechanisms. And the whole comparison rests on 30 answers."* — **Answered in part.** The length confound is answered directly and the thesis should lead with it: the first half of an answer is **not** significantly shorter than the second (mean 19.19 vs 18.39 words; difference 0.81 words, 95% CI [−2.28, 3.53]), and the correlation between claim position and claim length is −0.195 (p = 0.0003, n = 345) — later claims are *slightly shorter*, so "keep the first claims" cannot be a proxy for "keep the short claims". The second objection is **not** fully answered and must be conceded: the position advantage was measured on one generator's answers, on 30 of 32 answers with 345 claims, and it did **not** reproduce on the retrieved protocol (p = 0.417) or on the industrial replication (p = 0.500). The defensible form of the finding is therefore the *general* one, which is stronger and safer: **a confidence band whose ordering is indistinguishable from an independent reference on the gold protocol (κ 0.056; band error rates 0.542 / 0.513 / 0.610) cannot be acted on as a ranking, and where it is used at all it should be used as a binary filter with its retention cost stated.** The positional baseline is the evidence for that, not the finding itself.

## 5. Finding 4 — Point-in-time regulatory corpora are a source of ground-truth-labelled conflicts

**The claim, as the thesis would publish it.** The eCFR versioner API serves any US federal regulation as it stood on any past date. Federal regulations are amended, so the same section read at two dates is the same rule at two issues, and where the amendment changed a limit the two versions state **different requirements**. That yields conflicts whose provenance is known by construction and which need no synthetic injection and no expert comparison study: the thesis's industrial corpus pairs **2021-12-31 with 2026-06-01**, and the conflict *type* of a pair is known from which side of the amendment it sits on.

**The thesis's own artefacts** (`impl/data/corpus_industrial/manifest.json`, `impl/experiments/build_revision_candidates.py`, `impl/results_industrial/exp14_pairs.json`): 110 documents, four regimes (occupational safety 44, environmental control 37, hazmat transport 19, process safety 10), 2,443 chunks, public domain, eCFR versioner date 2026-06-01; two snapshots, `pre_date = 2021-12-31`, `post_date = 2026-06-01`; a candidate pool of **240 pairs** — a **90-pair revision stratum** (paragraphs of the same section aligned across the two snapshots whose text changed; 55 supersessions and **35 that change a numeric limit**) and a **150-pair retriever-neighbour stratum** mined by dense similarity alone, deliberately *not* by the detector's own rules; human annotation of all 240 (`supersession` 84, `term_mismatch` 90, `no_conflict` 64, `granularity` 2); and a separate quantity stratum of **1,776 cross-document quantity-conflict candidates** over 64 measurement terms, exhaustive over the corpus, on which the quantity branch fired 13 times and a blind specialist confirmed **0 of the 60 pairs shown**.

**What the literature already establishes.** **This finding has the most prior art of the five, and the thesis must say so plainly: reading a statute at two dates and using the version difference as a label is published.** (i) *A retrieval corpus of every dated version of every statute section, with by-construction in-force labels — published, nine days before this file.* `arXiv:2611.03576` (*NitiBench+: A Temporal Thai Legal Question Answering Benchmark Across 17 Domains*, 2 Nov 2026, **under review**) reports that it "checked 156,650 decisions of four Thai courts", that "up to 67% of them cite law that has since been amended or repealed", that "for each cited section it holds both the text in force on the decision date and the current text, and **its retrieval corpus holds every dated version of every Thai statute section**", and quantifies the cost of using the wrong text: "reading the current text of amended law drops the Bar-standard score by 0.25 and missing repealed law drops it by 0.79". (ii) *Versioned statutory QA with pre-/post-amendment questions as an evaluation set — published.* `arXiv:2605.23497` (*Asking For An Old Friend: Diagnosing and Mitigating Temporal Failure Modes in LLM-based Statutory Question Answering*, **ICAIL 2026**, nominated for Best Paper) presents "a benchmark of 312 expert-validated, time-sensitive German statutory QA pairs spanning three categories: Post-Cutoff Amendment Questions, Pre-Amendment Questions, and Multi-Provision Pre-Amendment Questions", evaluates five LLMs under two retrieval-augmented variants "that enforce temporal validity via a fact date extraction and version filtering", and finds "severe degradation in the Vanilla po[int-in-time] condition"; its two named failure modes — post-cutoff staleness and recency bias — are exactly the two a versioned corpus is designed to expose. (iii) *Which version of a statute applies, as a formalised task — published.* `arXiv:2608.14610` (*When Do LLMs Apply the Wrong Law?*) formalises **Temporal Applicable-Law Determination (TALD)** and builds **TALD-Bench** over "51,956 Chinese civil judgments spanning the enactment of the Civil Code", finding "a strong bias toward newer law, achieving over 70% accuracy on new-law cases but below 25% on old-law cases"; `arXiv:2510.06002` (SAT-Graph) models "legal materials as structure-aware temporal knowledge graphs" so that retrieval preserves "the hierarchy, temporality, and causal provenance of legal norms". (iv) *Temporal conflict and staleness as benchmark problems — published, in several neighbouring forms.* `arXiv:2510.19172` (evolveQA), `arXiv:2603.15892` (*Temporal Fact Conflicts in LLMs: Reproducibility Insights from Unifying DYNAMICQA and MULAN*), `arXiv:2609.03588` (KC-Bench), `arXiv:2608.29175` (*An Explainable Coherence Score for Detecting Temporal Inconsistencies in Political News*), and — from the project's own earlier survey — `arXiv:2604.14227` (FRESCO), which shows rerankers "a strong bias toward older, semantically rich documents, even when they are factually obsolete" using Wikipedia revisions. (v) *Conflict-type benchmarks in law, built the other two ways — published.* The two alternative constructions for a legal conflict benchmark are both in the literature: **expert-grounded comparison studies** (`arXiv:2608.28607`, RegDivergence-101, whose labels are "grounded in three peer-reviewed FDA/EMA comparison studies and primary FDA [sources]") and **controlled synthetic injection** (`arXiv:2510.03418`, LegalWiz). The thesis's construction is a third: take the conflicts from the regulation's own amendment history.

**Nearest prior work, and the verdict.** Nearest is `arXiv:2611.03576` (NitiBench+) for the corpus construction, `arXiv:2605.23497` (ICAIL 2026) for the by-construction pre-/post-amendment evaluation set, `arXiv:2608.14610` for the formalised "which version applies" task. *Same number?* No — NitiBench+ reports 1,670 items over 17 domains and the 0.25 / 0.79 score penalties; the thesis reports 240 pairs, 1,776 quantity candidates, 35 construction-labelled limit changes. *Same claim?* **Partly, and this must be conceded** — "read the statute at two dates and the difference is the label" is published, in Thai law, German law and Chinese law, so the thesis's claim that *point-in-time regulatory corpora are a source of ground-truth-labelled conflicts* is, **as a method**, known. *Occupies the position?* **No** — all three published works use versioned statute corpora to build **question–answer** benchmarks, to test *which law the model applies*; none uses them to build a **conflict-detection** benchmark, where the object of study is a pair of passages that disagree and the disagreement's *type* is the ground truth. **Verdict: new as a purpose, known as a method.** The corpus-construction method must be cited as prior art and must not be claimed; what the thesis contributes is a *use* of it — a conflict-detection corpus whose conflicts are real regulatory supersessions rather than injected or expert-assembled — and the measurement that a detector finds those conflicts (detection 0.659) and cannot type them (0.073).

**Residual novelty, in one sentence.** The published temporal-legal benchmarks use versioned statutes to test *which law a model applies*; the thesis uses versioned regulations to test *whether a detector can tell that two real passages of regulatory text disagree, and which kind of disagreement it is* — a construction method known in the temporal-legal-QA literature, pointed at a task the literature has not pointed it at.

**The strongest reviewer objection, and whether the evidence answers it.** *"NitiBench+ shipped on 2 November and already keeps every dated version of every statute section in a retrieval corpus, with the in-force text paired against the current text. 'Asking For An Old Friend' already builds a benchmark whose labels come from amendments. You have re-implemented a published method in a different jurisdiction and a different language, and what you have added is 240 hand-adjudicated pairs from one corpus. That is not a contribution to the field."* — **Answered only in part, and the concession must be explicit.** What the evidence answers: the *published* constructions differ in what they are used for. NitiBench+ grades answers against the Bar-standard rubric and measures the cost of using the wrong text; the thesis's corpus supplies **pairs of passages that disagree** to a detector, and it is the detector — not a question-answering model — that is under evaluation. The quantity stratum (1,776 candidates, 64 measurement terms, exhaustive over the corpus, 13 fires, 0 confirmed) and the revision stratum (35 pairs where a limit demonstrably changed) are *not* annotated question sets at all; they are candidate pools whose labels come from the corpus's own version history. What the evidence does not answer: the reviewer is right that the *method* is prior art and that one corpus, in one jurisdiction, one language and one regulatory family is a thin benchmark. The thesis's answer is not that the corpus is larger but that **the detector result is what the corpus was for** — and the detector result is the finding, not the corpus. If the corpus were the contribution, the objection would be fatal. It is not.

## 6. Finding 5 — BM25 beats the dense encoder on regulatory text

**The claim, as the thesis would publish it.** On 247 answerable queries over 110 US federal regulatory documents (2,443 chunks), the lexical leg carries the whole pipeline: BM25 Recall@10 **0.9595** against the dense encoder's **0.8785**, Δ = **−0.081**, paired Wilcoxon **p = 0.00026**, d_z = 0.238. Hybrid reciprocal-rank fusion **does not beat its own lexical leg** (Δ = 0.000, p = 1.000), and the category pre-filter is **a no-op on recall** (Δ = 0.000, p = 1.000; MRR +0.007, p = 0.088). On regulatory prose, the two mechanisms added on top of a lexical baseline buy nothing measurable.

**The thesis's own numbers** (`impl/results_industrial/exp1_ablation.json`; n = 247 answerable of 444 queries):

| Arm | R@5 | R@10 | MRR | nDCG@10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. BM25 only | 0.8785 | **0.9595** | 0.7554 | 0.8026 |
| B. Dense only | 0.8300 | 0.8785 | 0.6600 | 0.7104 |
| C. Hybrid (RRF) | 0.9312 | 0.9595 | 0.7711 | 0.8160 |
| D. + reranker | 0.9757 | 0.9879 | 0.8269 | 0.8671 |
| E. + category filter | 0.9717 | 0.9879 | 0.8339 | 0.8724 |

**What the literature already establishes.** **"BM25 beats an off-the-shelf dense encoder on legal and regulatory text" is established, in several jurisdictions, and the thesis must say so in the first line of this section.** (i) *On legal text — established.* `arXiv:2506.12895` (*Assessing the Performance Gap Between Lexical and Semantic Models for Information Retrieval With Formulaic Legal Language*) is the nearest work of all: on Court of Justice of the European Union passage retrieval, with "three complementary metrics", "**BM25 performs better than the dense models in more nuanced scenarios where repetition and verbatim quotes are less prevalent and in longer queries**", and "BM25 is a strong baseline, **surpassing off-the-shelf dense models in 4 out of 7 performance metrics**"; the same paper reports the countervailing mechanism the thesis should cite — "**fine-tuning a dense model on domain-specific data** led to improved performance, surpassing BM25 in most metrics". `arXiv:2412.07462` (Bilingual BSARD, statutory article retrieval in Dutch and French) reports "**BM25 remains a competitive baseline** compared to many zero-shot dense models in both languages". `arXiv:2604.06098` (JUÁ, Brazilian legal IR) evaluates "lexical, dense, and BM25-based reranking pipelines" and reports that "**BM25 remains highly competitive on other collections, especially in settings with strong lexical and institutional phrasing cues**". `arXiv:2608.18752` (GreekBarRetrieval, **NLLP 2026**) is the **counter-case that must be reported**: "vanilla dense retrieval far outperforms vanilla sparse retrieval in Recall@100", though "LLM-based query reformulation helps BM25 close that gap" and a ten-round reformulation loop gives BM25 "the best nDCG and MAP scores of all tested retrievers". (ii) *On financial text-and-table documents — established.* `arXiv:2604.01733` (*From BM25 to Corrective RAG*) benchmarks ten retrieval strategies over "23,088 queries over 7,318 documents with mixed text-and-table content" and reports that "**BM25 outperforms state-of-the-art dense retrieval on financial documents, challenging the common assumption that semantic search universally dominat[es]**". (iii) *That hybrid fusion over a lexical baseline does not reliably pay — established.* `arXiv:2610.37358` (*When Is Additional Retrieval Worthwhile?*) reports, on 60 held-out QASPER articles and 188 questions, that its hybrid-vs-dense policy "triggers hybrid retrieval on [46] questions" and that "paired, paper-cluster bootstrap intervals do not establish a reliable utility advantage"; the opposite outcome is published too, on a different legal task, in `arXiv:2606.03138` (Section-Weighted Hybrid Approach for Legal Case Retrieval), which shows RRF-fused BM25 + dense ANN with learned section weights "demonstrating consistent gains over strong lexical and neural baselines". (iv) *That lexical retrieval is the strongest scalable default — established.* `arXiv:2607.26497` (*BM25 Wins at Scale*) runs a controlled scaling study over "28 strictly nested tiers spanning roughly 450-fold", finds "a scale-dependent crossover rather than an unconditional winner", and reports that "**Dense retrieval remains efficient but less accurate**" and "lexical retrieval is the strongest scalable default"; `arXiv:2104.08663` (BEIR) is the heterogeneous-corpus baseline in which BM25 is hard to beat. (v) *That the thesis's own dense leg is the weak one — established, and this is the point.* `arXiv:2604.17778` (TeleEmbedBench) exists precisely because "general-purpose benchmarks fail to capture the dense, acronym-heavy, and highly cross-referential nature of telecommunications corpora"; `arXiv:2606.02814` (*Do Neural Retrievers Prefer Certain Documents?*) adds the mechanism — supervised dense retrievers encode a query-independent relevance prior producing a "findability gap", so "documents with lower prior are systematically harder to retrieve, even when genuinely relevant".

**Nearest prior work, and the verdict.** Nearest is `arXiv:2506.12895` (lexical-vs-semantic gap on formulaic legal language), `arXiv:2604.01733` (BM25 over dense on financial text-and-table), `arXiv:2412.07462` (BM25 competitive on statutory retrieval), `arXiv:2608.18752` for the counter-case. *Same number?* No — the published BM25-over-dense margins are on other corpora, metrics and encoders; Δ = −0.081 at p = 0.00026 on 247 regulatory queries is the thesis's own measurement. *Same claim?* **Yes, in substance** — "BM25 beats an off-the-shelf dense retriever on legal / regulatory / financial text" is published in at least three jurisdictions and one financial corpus, and the thesis cannot claim this. *Occupies the position?* **Yes, for the BM25 leg** — the three published legal retrieval benchmarks (`2506.12895`, `2412.07462`, `2604.06098`) already occupy "we measured lexical against dense on legal text"; the thesis's distinctive position is the **pipeline corollary**. **Verdict: already known as stated, and a boundary condition as extended.**

**Residual novelty, in one sentence.** The BM25-over-dense comparison is published in legal and financial text; what is not published, and what the thesis measures, is that the fusion and the metadata pre-filter **add nothing on top of that lexical baseline** (Δ 0.000, p = 1.000 each), so the pipeline's floor is the lexical leg and the reranker alone.

**The strongest reviewer objection, and whether the evidence answers it.** *"You used a 2022 six-layer MiniLM as your dense leg. TeleEmbedBench exists precisely because general-purpose sentence-transformers fail on standards text, and 'Assessing the Performance Gap' found that a dense model fine-tuned on domain data beats BM25 on most metrics. GreekBarRetrieval found plain dense beating plain sparse on statutory retrieval. You have shown that BM25 beats a weak baseline on a formulaic corpus — that is a finding about your embedder, not about dense retrieval."* — **Answered only in part, and the concession must be explicit.** What the evidence answers: the thesis's negative results are consistent with the published picture rather than in tension with it (`2604.01733` the same ordering on financial text-and-table, `2412.07462` on statutory retrieval, `2604.06098` across Brazilian legal collections), and the fusion and pre-filter results (Δ 0.000, p = 1.000 each) do not depend on the embedder at all: the lexical leg's recall is 0.9595 and nothing added on top of it changed recall. What the evidence does **not** answer: Δ = −0.081 is a margin over a **2022 general-purpose encoder**, so it bounds this pipeline, not dense retrieval as a class. The result must therefore be published as a **boundary condition on the thesis's own pipeline** — "the lexical leg carries this corpus, and the two mechanisms added on top of it were worth nothing measurable" — with `2604.17778` and `2506.12895` cited for the stronger statement that a *domain-adapted* dense encoder can overtake BM25. The honest reading is that the thesis has measured the floor of its own system, found it lexical, and identified raising it as future work (`research/09_encoder_swap.md`).

## 8. What this changes about the thesis's positioning

**A correction, applied after this assessment was written.** This file
judged the industrial replication's non-replication from the *first*
replication run, which had 73 claims from a different generator and was
underpowered. The replication was then re-run with the same generator that
produced the focus corpus's answers, on all 247 answerable questions, and
the numbers changed: the positional comparison is p = 0.083 (d_z = +0.328) on
the industrial gold protocol and p = 0.317 on the retrieved one, against
p = 0.417 on the focus corpus's retrieved protocol — so **one of four
corpus-and-protocol combinations is significant, not zero of four**. The
verdict on Finding 3 stands: the *filter* result is published and the
*ranking* result is the new part, and the positional comparison is evidence
for the agreement-versus-correctness reading rather than the finding itself.
The agreement-versus-correctness split, by contrast, holds on all four
combinations (industrial gold κ 0.062, industrial retrieved κ 0.044, both
intervals including zero, with the bands ordered on the evidence they saw).

**The one-line version.** Of the five findings, **one is a new measurement** (Finding 2), **one is a new measurement with a published mechanism around it** (Finding 3), **one is a sharper version of a published principle** (Finding 1), **one is a known method pointed at a new purpose** (Finding 4), and **one is already known** (Finding 5). The thesis's positioning has to be rebuilt around that distribution, not around "five new findings".

**Claims that must be softened.**

- *"BM25 beats dense retrieval on industrial/regulatory text."* Already published, three times over (`arXiv:2506.12895`, `arXiv:2604.01733`, `arXiv:2412.07462`) — **do not put this in the abstract**. Restate as: *on this corpus, with a general-purpose 2022 encoder, the lexical leg carried the pipeline, and the fusion and the category pre-filter added nothing measurable on top of it.* That is a statement about the thesis's own pipeline, and it is true; the current statement is not new.
- *"Domain-specialised LLM annotator panels may be buying agreement rather than accuracy."* The qualitative form is published (`arXiv:2412.14461`, `arXiv:2605.20809`, `arXiv:2611.10048`). Restate as: *the specification of the task moved the measured precision of an unchanged detector by a factor of nine.* If the thesis keeps the panel framing at all, it must say that "specialisation" was delivered by a brief to a fixed reader model.
- *"Point-in-time regulatory corpora are a source of ground-truth-labelled conflicts."* The *construction method* is published (NitiBench+, ICAIL 2026, TALD). Restate as: *we applied a published corpus-construction method to conflict detection, and the detector that consumed it finds the conflicts and cannot type them.*
- *Per-claim confidence tagging as a mechanism.* Already published as a mechanism family (`arXiv:2610.34872`, `arXiv:2609.04442`, `arXiv:2305.14627`), consistent with `research/07`'s existing verdict. The finding sharpens it rather than adding to it.

**Claims that must be sharpened — these are the paper's spine.**

- **The detection-versus-typing decomposition.** This is the cleanest new thing in the thesis: a *metric* contribution (report a conflict detector's precision as two numbers), a *result* (they are nine-fold apart), and a *specification* (a detector for regulatory text needs version metadata, or must be scoped to detection only). Pair it with the quantity branch's recall of **0.000** on 35 construction-labelled limit changes, which is the crispest negative result in the whole thesis — a null against a stratum where the answer is present by construction.
- **The band measures agreement with itself, not correctness.** Ordered on the evidence it saw (0.118 / 0.103 / 0.220), indistinguishable on an independent reference (0.542 / 0.513 / 0.610; κ 0.056 [−0.009, 0.121]). That is a sharper and safer statement than "position beats the band", which did not replicate on the retrieved protocol or on the industrial corpus. **Publish the general claim and report the positional comparison as its evidence, not as the finding.**
- **The rubric factorial.** The factor-of-nine is a *methodological* result that belongs in a paper's evaluation section, not in its contribution list — but it is also the kind of result reviewers reward.

**Findings that could be a paper on their own.**

1. **Best shape: Findings 2 + 4 together** — a conflict-detection benchmark built from point-in-time regulatory corpora, with the detection/typing decomposition as its headline metric. This is the most paper-shaped item in the thesis: a construction method (cited, not claimed), a benchmark, and a negative result about an operating detector. It is also the one that survives a reviewer who has read NitiBench+, because the method is prior art and the contribution is the task and the metric.
2. **Finding 3** — a short negative-result paper: "a per-claim confidence mechanism that a positional heuristic beats is not a confidence mechanism to act on", with the informative-versus-agreement decomposition as the second contribution. Requires either a larger corpus or an honest "did not replicate" section.
3. **Finding 1** — a workshop-scale methodological paper on rubric-versus-specialisation in LLM annotator panels, with the two-profile detector-precision measurement as the hook. Needs a larger pair set; the current 240 pairs and 41 alarms will not survive review as a headline factor.

**What must not be a paper.** Finding 5. It is a null result on the thesis's own pipeline. It belongs in the ablation table and in the limitations, and it is the strongest argument for reporting the false-positive rate of the category pre-filter (`research/08`, T1). Trying to make it a paper would be the one place where the thesis inflates novelty, and `arXiv:2604.01733` would end it.

**One thing that has to change in the deliverables, not just in the prose.** RO3's hypothesis — hybrid + RRF + category pre-filter beats flat retrieval — is contradicted by the thesis's own numbers on both corpora (fusion Δ 0.000, p = 1.000; pre-filter Δ 0.000, p = 1.000 on recall, MRR +0.007, p = 0.088 on the industrial corpus). It must be rewritten as a negative-result hypothesis before 18 November, and `research/08` §4 T1 already says so. The benchmark shortcut audit (`impl/results_industrial/exp12_unanswerable_class_audit.json`) is the model for how the rest of the results should be presented: the shortcut named, the number reported.
